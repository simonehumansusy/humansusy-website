Critical challenges of AI adoption

Some organisations haven't decided anything yet, while their people already use AI on their own. Others are experimenting, rolling out, or have rolled out and are still waiting for the results. The stage differs, but the problems come back to the same few.

Rules

In most organisations, AI is already in use through sector software or personal accounts on tools like ChatGPT, but without shared rules on what data goes in, which output to trust and who checks it. That creates quality risks and exposure under the EU AI Act and GDPR.

Results

Efficiency was promised, but AI tools require supervision and generate additional tasks and complexity. Sometimes they end up in silent non-use.

Management

Management is expected to lead the change in their teams, without having been equipped for it. Most managers are figuring out AI themselves while being asked to guide others.

Workload

When AI saves time, it often turns into more volume rather than better work. Roles change as tasks shift to AI, and people need clarity on what is now expected of them and which skills to keep developing.

Return

Adoption stays below target and the return is hard to prove. Without a baseline, nobody can show whether the investment paid off, and it can end up negative.

The real cost of AI doesn't sit only in the licence, but especially in the time, attention and change it asks from people. A wrong use case wastes all of it.

From the work to the technology

Most AI consulting starts from the tool and then manages the resistance. I start from the work: the tasks, the judgement they require, the workload, the skills and who decides. Only then do we choose the technology and design how it is introduced. Adoption mostly fails for people reasons (unclear rules, overload, fear, missing skills, being left out of the decision), and those only become visible when you look at the work first.

AI pays back only when three conditions hold, so I assess them together.

Business value

Which tasks matter for cost, quality or growth, what improving them is worth, and what it costs, including people's time.

Processes and data

Whether the workflow is stable and the data reliable enough for AI to work on. AI on a fragile process or poor data fails, whatever the tool.

People

Whether adoption builds people up or uses them up, across five dimensions:

  • capability: are skills and judgement growing or eroding?
  • participation: do the people affected shape how AI is introduced?
  • equilibrium: does workload stay sustainable, or does freed time become more volume?
  • belonging: do teams adopt together, or is everyone figuring it out alone?
  • equity: are benefits and costs shared fairly across roles?
A use case that is valuable and technically ready still fails if people don't adopt it, or if it erodes the skills it depends on.

The five dimensions come from my research on human sustainability, built on documented AI adoption cases across industries: what worked, what didn't, and why.
